The Schrodinger equation is a statement of the conservation of energy:
total energy = kinetic energy + potential energy, E = T + V

Total energy from the wavefunction: $E\psi = j\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}$
Momentum from the wavefunction: $p\psi = - j\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$
Kinetic energy from the wavefunction: $T\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}$

Time dependent Schrodinger equation:
$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta^2 \psi(r,t)+V(r,t)\psi(r,t)=j\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(r,t)$
Time independent Schrodinger equation:
$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta^2 \psi(r)+V(r)\psi(r)=E\psi(r)$

If we have problems with 3 spatial dimensions, we replace $r$ with $\textbf{r}$

Postulates of quantum mechanics -
* For a particle in a potential, there is an associated wavefunction which determines everything that can be known about the particle
* The wavefunction satisfies Schrodingers equation at all times
* The wavefunction is a complex, single-valued function and must be continuous, as must its spatial derivatives
* The probability of finding the particle in a volume element $\partial x\partial y\partial z$ about a point $(x,y,z)$ is:
  $P(x,y,z)=|\psi|^2\partial x \partial y \partial z$

Uncertainty principle - To measure the wavelength with infinite accuracy, we would need an infinitely extended wavepacket, which implies the particle could be anywhere
Conversely, if we know exactly where a particle is, then its wavepacket must be infinitely small, so we cannot know its wavelength and hence momentum

If $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ are the uncertainties in position and momentum respectively, then $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$
Similary, as $E=hf$, this implies $\Delta E \Delta t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$

We can see we have limited number of states, like the resonant waves on a string, and energy is quantised.

The electron is orbiting the nucleus with an angular momentum $\textbf{L}$
The magnitude of the electron's orbital angular momentum is given by $L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\hbar$
The component of the electron's orbital angular momentum along the z direction is given by $L_z=m_l \hbar$

Quantum mechanics forbids us from simultaneously know the components in the other two directions ($L_x$ and $L_y$)

Interpretation -
There are discrete states in which the electron can exist, which are denoted by three quantum numbers: $n,l$ and $m_l$
$l$ and $m_l$ determine the angular momentum of the electron. $l$ describes the orbital shape, $m_l$ determines its orientation. $n$ determines the energy
For simplicity the states are denoted by a number followed by a letter - $1s,2s,2p,...$

Conditions -
$n$ is a positive integer $> 0$
$l$ is an integer from $0$ to $n-1$, so $l<n$
$m_l$ is an integer, positive or negative, $|m_l|\leq l$

Pauli Exclusion Principle - 
In a multi electron system there can never be more than one electron in the same state
