Electron diffraction - Bragg diffraction
Let's consider a beam of X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ impinging on a regular crystal structure at an angle $\theta$
Constructive interference happens when $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$ where $d$ is the lattice spacing

$\psi$ is the wavefunction of the particle
$f(x-vt)$ forward moving
$f(x+vt)$ backward moving

Wavenumber - $k=2\pi / \lambda$

Different types of waves and the form of their $\psi(x,t)$:
Standing wave - $A\cos kx \cos \omega t$ or $A\cos kx \exp^{-j\omega t}$
Travelling wave - $A\cos (kx - \omega t)$ or $A \exp^{j(kx - \omega t)}$
Wavepacket - $\sum_i A_i cos (k_ix - \omega_i t)$ or $\sum_i A_i e^ {j(k_ix - \omega_i t)}$

The Schrodinger equation is a statement of the conservation of energy:
total energy = kinetic energy + potential energy, E = T + V

Total energy from the wavefunction: $E\psi = j\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}$
Momentum from the wavefunction: $p\psi = - j\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$
Kinetic energy from the wavefunction: $T\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}$

Time dependent Schrodinger equation:
$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta^2 \psi(r,t)+V(r,t)\psi(r,t)=j\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(r,t)$
Time independent Schrodinger equation:
$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta^2 \psi(r)+V(r)\psi(r)=E\psi(r)$

Postulates of quantum mechanics -
* For a particle in a potential, there is an associated wavefunction which determines everything that can be known about the particle
* The wavefunction satisfies Schrodingers equation at all times
* The wavefunction is a complex, single-valued function and must be continuous, as must its spatial derivatives
* The probability of finding the particle in a volume element $\partial x\partial y\partial z$ about a point $(x,y,z)$ is:
  $P(x,y,z)=|\psi|^2\partial x \partial y \partial z$

Uncertainty principle - To measure the wavelength with infinite accuracy, we would need an infinitely extended wavepacket, which implies the particle could be anywhere
Conversely, if we know exactly where a particle is, then its wavepacket must be infinitely small, so we cannot know its wavelength and hence momentum

If $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ are the uncertainties in position and momentum respectively, then $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$
This also implies $\Delta E \Delta t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$

If we consider a particle in an infinite potential well, and work it through, we get the solution:
$\psi=B\sin(\frac{\pi n}{L}x)$, where n is the quantum number
From normalisation, we require $\int_0^L |\psi|^2 dx = 1$
We can see we have limited number of states, like the resonant waves on a string, and energy is quantised.
The lowest possible energy state is $n=1$, the ground state

The Hydrogen Atom -
$V(r)=\frac{-e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r}$. To solve the TISE, we will need to put it in polar coordinates
This will have to be solved as a separable differential equation
The phi component solution: $Ae^{jm_l\phi}$, which repeated every $2\pi$
The theta component solution: The set of Legendre functions $P_{l,m_l}$
The radial r component: $\frac{f_{n,l}(r)}{r}exp(\frac{-r}{a})$

For the one electron system, it is the value of $n$ alone which determines the energy of the electron $E_n=\frac{-e^4 m}{8\epsilon_0^2h^2n^2}$

Interpretation -
There are discrete states in which the electron can exist, which are denoted by three quantum numbers: $n,l$ and $m_l$
$l$ and $m_l$ determine the angular momentum of the electron whereas $n$ determines the energy
For simplicity the states are denoted by a number followed by a letter - $1s,2s,2p,...$

Pauli Exclusion Principle - 
In a multi electron system there can never be more than one electron in the same state
Electrons have a fourth quantum number $m_s$, the spin. For electrons: $m_s=\pm 1/2$
It's important for describing electron behaviour in a magnetic field

The magnitude of the spin angular momentum is given by $S=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{2})}\hbar\approx 0.87\hbar$
$m_s$ determines the direction of the spin angular momentum, the component in the z direction. $S_z=m_s\hbar=\pm \frac{1}{2}\hbar$

It is for this reason that not all elements are noble gases, but have preferred numbers of electrons and hence bond together to make molecules

The lowest energy level, the ground state, is the 1s level (n=1, l=0, $m_l=0$). An electron in this level can be spin up or spin down depending on $m_s$
For atoms of higher atomic number, the interaction between electrons means that the energy of an electron is now dependent on both $n$ and $l$

Electrons will occupy the lowest available energy levels in an atom
$p$ orbitals can fit 6 electrons

Energy band formation -
If two atoms are brought close together such that the two $3p$ wavefunctions overlap, then the Pauli exclusion principle is invoked.

We get band splitting into a conduction band on top, and a bandgap to the valence band on the bottom
For a semiconductor like silicon, as T increases, the number of electron-hole pairs increases and the conductivity of the semiconductor rises. For a pure semiconductor, the number of electrons = number of holes = intrinsic carrier concentration
$$n=p=n_i$$
Metals have overlapping bands

As the thickness of gate dielectric is decreases, we would expect to see an exponential increase in tunnelling current.

In order to predict and control the electrical behaviour of materials, we need to know:
* How many electron states there are at a given energy
* How many of these states are occupied by electrons

Free electron model -
Assumes electrons behave like a gas of free particles (so no interaction between them), and that the removal of a valence electron to be a free carrier leaves behind a positively charged ionic core, and the associated charge density due to these ionic cores is spread uniformly throughout the solid, with potential $V_0$

This is quite similar to the infinite potential well, except now it's 3D box with finite potential barriers
In one dimension, we get an E-k relation, $E=\frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}$
It is quasi-continuous because k takes on discrete values defined by integer n, but there are so many closely spaced discrete states that it approaches a continuous curve

Fermi-Dirac Statistics -
We don't yet know which of these states are actually occupied by an electron
Consider a situation where we have N free electrons and temperature is 0K. From Pauli Exclusion principle, once the lowest energy state is filled, the electrons are forced to occupy higher energy states

At absolute zero, we define the Fermi energy $E_F$ to be the energy of the highest filled state
$f(E)= 1$ if $E\leq E_F$ or $0$ if $E>E_F$ where $f(E)$ is probability of occupance

N states will be occupied:
$N=\int_0^{\infty}g(E)f(E)dE=\int_0^{E_F}g(E)dE$ where $g(E)$ is density of states

As the temperature rises above absolute zero, some electrons within an energy of $\approx k_BT$ of the Fermi energy have sufficient energy to enter states above $E_F$
$f(E)=\frac{1}{exp(\frac{E-E_F}{k_B T})+1}$

In semiconductors, the internal crystal structure becomes important, so we need the nearly free electron model -
The assumption of a constant homogeneous lattice potential is not valid
We must consider a set of positively charged nuclei arranged in an ordered fashion, with a potential will associated with each nucleus

We will need to change our model in two ways:
1) Forbidden energy gaps in the E-k curve
2) Periodicity in the E-k curve

The solution to the free electron model was a plane wave in one direction -
$\psi = Aexp(jkx)exp(-j\omega t)$ where A is a constant and $k=2\pi / \lambda$

A wave can pass through a diffraction grating of spacing a unless a is an integer number of half wavelengths $a=\frac{n\lambda}{2}$
If the wavelength of the electron does not meet the Bragg reflection criteria, the travelling plane wave solution will apply to a first approximation

If it does not, then it will be Bragg reflected. Every Bragg reflection reverses the direction of travel of the wave. The wave then becomes a standing wave that has locked on to the periodicity of the lattice. The electron cannot propagate as a travelling wave
Bragg reflection occurs when $k=\pm \frac{n\pi}{a}$

Two possibilities in this case -
1) Low probability of the electron being near the nuclei. Such a state will have a higher energy, $E=E_{free}+\Delta E$ than the free energy theory predicts
2) High probability of the electron being near the nuclei
![[Pasted image 20260923160637.png]]
Such a state will have a lower energy $E=E_{free}-\Delta E$

Energy gap of $E_g=2\Delta E$ between the two states. Thus, an energy gap occurs whenever $k\approx \pm n\pi/a$

The effect of the periodic lattice is to open up periodic energy gaps - band gaps - in the E-k diagram

The k-axis has also been split into several well-defined regions known as Brillouin zones. The first Brillouin zone corresponds to the lowest energy band, the second to the next lowest band and so on

The Bloch's theorem -
Allows us to further simplify the situation
"The wavefunction of an electron in a periodic potential is a plane wave times a function with the periodicity of the lattice"
$\psi = Aexp(jkx)u(x)$

Semiconductors where the bottom of the conduction band has the sane k as the top of the valence band are known as direct gap semiconductors - an electron only requires a change in energy to make a transition between the two
For an indirect gap semiconductor, an electron will require energy and the addition of momentum

This is why indirect semiconductors make inefficient optoelectronic devices as photons have almost no momentum. An interaction with lattice vibrations (phonons) would also be required

Effective mass of an electron varies as $m^*=\hbar^2(\frac{d^2E}{dk^2})^{-1}$

In a completely full energy band, the sum of all the electronic $k$ vectors is zero, as no net motion of electrons in those bands
There are three ways of transferring electrons into the CB:
1) Thermal excitation
2) Atomic substitution or doping
3) Photoexcitation - absorption of a photon

The law of mass action -
Even if we dope the material and change n or p, the product of the two always remains the same for a given semiconductor at a constant temperature. $np=n_i$
