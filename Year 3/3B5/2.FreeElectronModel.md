In order to predict and control the electrical behaviour of materials, we need to know:
* How many electron states there are at a given energy
* How many of these states are occupied by electrons

Free electron model -
Assumes electrons behave LIKE A GAS OF FREE PARTICLES (so no interaction between them), and that the removal of a valence electron to be a free carrier leaves behind a positively charged ionic core, and the associated charge density due to these ionic cores is spread uniformly throughout the solid, with potential $V_0$
![[Pasted image 20261023105313.png]]

This is quite similar to the infinite potential well, except now it's 3D box with finite potential barriers
As potentials are relative, we set the potential inside the well as the origin $V_0=0$

The solution is a plane wave $\psi=Aexp(j\textbf{k}\cdot \textbf{r})exp(-j\omega t)$, where $\textbf{r}=(x,y,z)$
A is a normalising constant
$\textbf{k}=(k_x,k_y,k_z)$, the wavevector of the electron and is related to its momentum vector: $\textbf{p}=\hbar \textbf{k}$

The wavenumber is just the magnitude of the wavevector

The boundary conditions are chosen to allow travelling wave solutions: $\psi(x,y,z)=\phi(x+L_x,y+L_y, z+L_z)$
This means that $k_x=\pm \frac{2\pi}{L_x}n_x$ and similar for y and z. $n_i$ are positive integers 

$n_i$ are analogous to the principal quantum number $n$ and hence so is the wavevector

We can map out these wavevectors as a grid of points in $\textbf{k}-$space, called the momentum space or reciprocal space since $k\propto 1/L$
The electron can only exist in one of a number of discrete states in the reciprocal space

Subbing the wavefunction into the TISE we get that $E=\frac{\hbar^2 |\textbf{k}|^2}{2m}$

In one dimension, we get an E-k relation, $E=\frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}$, giving and E-k curve

It is quasi-continuous because k takes on discrete values defined by integer n, but there are so many closely spaced discrete states that it approaches a continuous curve

In the reciprocal space, all points on spherical shells have the same energy

Density of states -
Remember $k_i = \pm \frac{2\pi}{L_i}n_i$
The volume of reciprocal space per point is $\frac{2\pi}{L_x}\times \frac{2\pi}{L_y}\times \frac{2\pi}{L_z} = \frac{8\pi^3}{V}$ where V is real space volume
We have two electrons at each point, so the density of state  is $2\frac{V}{8\pi^3}$

The number of states is therefore $\frac{2V}{8\pi^3}d^3\textbf{k}$

Remembering that a shell in reciprocal space is all the wavevectors with the same wavenumber. The SA is $4\pi k^2$ and volume is $4\pi k^2 dk$

The number of states between k and k+dk: $n(k)dk = \frac{2V}{8\pi^3}4\pi k^2 dk = \frac{V}{\pi^2}k^2dk$
It's more natural to work in terms of electron energy so we need $g(E)dE$

$k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$. Differentiate to find relation of dk to dE
$g(E)dE=\frac{V}{2\pi^2 \hbar^3}(2m)^{3/2}E^{1/2}dE$
The density of states increases with the square root of electron energy. The greater the energy, the more states available

Fermi-Dirac Statistics -
We don't yet know which of these states are actually occupied by an electron
Consider a situation where we have N free electrons and temperature is 0K. From Pauli Exclusion principle, only two electrons can take a particular wavevector, so once the lowest energy state is filled, the electrons are forced to occupy higher energy states.
N lowest states must be filled

At absolute zero, we define the Fermi energy $E_F$ to be the energy of the highest filled state
$f(E)= 1$ if $E\leq E_F$ or $0$ if $E>E_F$ where $f(E)$ is probability of occupance

N states will be occupied:
$N=\int_0^{\infty}g(E)f(E)dE=\int_0^{E_F}g(E)dE$ where $g(E)$ is density of states

We can sub in our expression for $g(E)$ and solve. $E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 \frac{N}{V})^{2/3}$, so dependent on N/V, the number of free electrons per unit volume

A typical volume for N/V is $10^{29} \text{m}^{-3}$ 

As the temperature rises above absolute zero, some electrons within an energy of $\approx k_BT$ of the Fermi energy have sufficient energy to enter states above $E_F$

We can use a Boltzmann distribution to determine the number of particles with a particular energy $n\propto exp(\frac{-E}{k_B T})$, provided we have infinite states. But we don't, so we have to use the Fermi-Dirac distribution for the probability that a state is occupied:

$f(E)=\frac{1}{exp(\frac{E-E_F}{k_B T})+1}$. Learn!!
Only works for fermions - particles with mass
If $E>>E_F$ this tends to the Boltzmann distribution

The Fermi level, $E_L$, marks the energy at which there is a 50% prob that a state is occupied!!!
Fermi energy is really only meant for 0K.
The Fermi level is sometimes called the chemical potential $\mu_c$

The number of electrons with a given energy $n(E)=g(E)f(E)$, and the total number of electrons is the integral from 0 to $\infty$ of this

This works well for metals, as the sea of electrons spreads out the potential, but cannot be applied to semiconductors

Nearly Free Electron Model -
In semiconductors, the internal crystal structure becomes important, so we need the nearly free electron model -
The assumption of a constant homogeneous lattice potential is not valid, as valence electrons in ionic/covalent bonds are localised near the parent atom.

We must consider a set of positively charged nuclei arranged in an ordered fashion, with a potential will associated with each nucleus
![[Pasted image 20260923154758.png]]

We will need to change our model in two ways:
1) Forbidden energy gaps in the E-k curve
2) Periodicity in the E-k curve

The solution to the free electron model was a plane wave in one direction -
$\psi = Aexp(jkx)exp(-j\omega t)$ where A is a constant and $k=2\pi / \lambda$

A wave can pass through a diffraction grating of spacing a unless a is an integer number of half wavelengths $a=\frac{n\lambda}{2}$ which is just Bragg's law with $\theta = 90\degree$. The periodic lattice acts like a diffraction grating.
If the wavelength of the electron does not meet the Bragg reflection criteria, the travelling plane wave solution will apply to a first approximation, and the electron keeps propagating as a travelling wave.

If it does not, then it will be Bragg reflected. Every Bragg reflection reverses the direction of travel of the wave. The wave then becomes a standing wave that has locked on to the periodicity of the lattice. The electron cannot propagate as a travelling wave
Bragg reflection occurs when $k=\pm \frac{n\pi}{a}$

Two possibilities in this case, no other options -
1) Low probability of the electron being near the nuclei. Such a state will have a higher energy, $E=E_{free}+\Delta E$ than the free energy theory predicts. Essentially the antibonding state, the electron is outside the potential well of one nucleus
2) High probability of the electron being near the nuclei
![[Pasted image 20260923160637.png]]
Such a state will have a lower energy $E=E_{free}-\Delta E$

Energy gap of $E_g=2\Delta E$ between the two states. Thus, an energy gap occurs whenever $k\approx \pm n\pi/a$. This is what causes the gaps in the E-k diagram

The effect of the periodic lattice is to open up periodic energy gaps - band gaps - in the E-k diagram
![[Pasted image 20260923161327.png]]
The k-axis has also been split into several well-defined regions known as Brillouin zones. The first Brillouin zone corresponds to the lowest energy band, the second to the next lowest band and so on. Everything can be referred to the 1st Brillouin zone, so it's the most important

The Bloch's theorem -
Allows us to further simplify the situation
Born-von Karman periodic boundary condition - $V(x)=V(x+Na)$
"The wavefunction of an electron in a periodic potential is a plane wave times a function with the periodicity of the lattice"
$\psi = Aexp(jkx)u(x)$

Consequence - 
We cannot distinguish between an electron with a wavenumber k and one with a wavenumber $k+n2\pi /a$
We can show this by substituting $k' = k+n2\pi /a$ into $\psi = Aexp(jkx)u(x)$ which will give us an equation which also satisfies Bloch's theorem
This means we can shift an point on an E-k diagram by $\pm n2\pi / a$ so can condense it all into first Brillouin zone
![[Pasted image 20261027113431.png]]
Only really need one half of this diagram as symmetric.
We also commonly plot in different crystallographic direction. Most of the time you are focusing on the lowest and closest band gap

Semiconductors where the bottom of the conduction band has the same k as the top of the valence band are known as direct gap semiconductors - an electron only requires a change in energy to make a transition between the two
For an indirect gap semiconductor, an electron will require energy and the addition of momentum

Silicon is an indirect gap semiconductor, so will make an inefficient optoelectrnic device

This is why indirect semiconductors make inefficient optoelectronic devices as photons have almost no momentum. An interaction with lattice vibrations (phonons) would also be required

Effective mass -
An electron in a periodic potential behaves differently to a free electron
$E=hf = \hbar \omega = 1/2 mv^2 + V(x)$
Differentiating gives $\partial \omega = \frac{mv}{\hbar} \partial v$, so $\partial v = \frac{\hbar}{m} \partial k$
$$v = \frac{\partial \omega}{\partial k}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E}{\partial k}$$
Subbing this into the Newtonian $F=m^*\frac{dv}{dt}$ and considering force as the rate of change of momentum, we get that:
Effective mass of an electron varies as $m^*=\hbar^2(\frac{d^2E}{dk^2})^{-1}$

We use this when we want to use Newtonian mechanics in a quantum mechanical situation
![[Pasted image 20261027115516.png]]
Can never get right to the Bragg point as it would require infinite force

A material acts as a semiconductor if at 0K: filled VB, empty CB

In a completely full energy band, the sum of all the electronic $k$ vectors is zero, as no net motion of electrons in those bands
There are three ways of transferring electrons into the CB:
1) Thermal excitation - attain enough energy according to Fermi-Dirac distribution
2) Atomic substitution or doping
3) Photoexcitation - absorption of a photon provides energy

Excited electrons occupy bottom of CB. Unoccupied state near top of VB
Left with simplified E-k diagram
![[Pasted image 20260923170102.png]]
Energy at top of VB = $E_V$. Energy at bottom of CB = $E_C$. $E_g=E_C-E_V$
Important assumption - We set $E_V = 0$ as we always care about energy difference

Provided an external force by an applied electric field $\epsilon$: $F=-e\epsilon = m^*a$
The effective mass $m_e$ of an electron near the top of the VB is negative due to the curvature of the E-k diagram. $m_h=-m_e$ so the hole should have **positive** effective mass

If a single electron has wavenumber $k_e$, when we lose an electron we're left with a hole with wavenumber $h_h = -k_e$. Similarly $E_h = - E_e$
Group velocity of hole $v_h = \frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial(-E_e)}{\partial (-k_e)}$ is the same as the group velocity of the electron

Applying an electric field $\epsilon$ will cause a change in hole momentum, $p_h$:
$F = e\epsilon$. It behaves as though it has a positive charge of $+e$

The law of mass action -
Number density of electrons in the CB - n
Number density of holes in the VB - p

According to free electron model, we found the density of states. Luckily, when considering the nearly free electron model, in this equation we only need to swap m with $m^*$
Density of states in the CB:
$g(E)\,\mathrm{d}E = \frac{V}{2\pi^2 \hbar^3} \left(2m_e^*\right)^{\frac{3}{2}} \left(E - E_C\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \mathrm{d}E$
Density of states in the VB:
$g(E)\,\mathrm{d}E = \frac{V}{2\pi^2 \hbar^3} \left(2m_h^*\right)^{\frac{3}{2}} \left(E_V - E\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \mathrm{d}E$
![[Pasted image 20261029122911.png]]
We need to multiply by probability that a state is occupied by an electron. This is given by the Fermi-Dirac distribution $f(E)$
The probability for holes is just $f_p(E)=1-f(E)$

$f_h(E) = 1 - f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\frac{-(E - E_F)}{k_B T}\right)}$

For conduction band:
Finding $n = \frac{1}{V}\int_{E_C}^{\infty}g(E)f(E)dE$, where we can approximate $f(E)$ as a Boltzmann distribution
This results in:
$$n = N_C e^{(\frac{E_F-E_C}{k_BT})}$$
$N_C$ is the effective density of states in the conduction band
$N_C = 2(\frac{m_e^* k_B T}{2\pi \hbar ^2})^{3/2}$

For valence band:
Using $f_h(E)=1-f(E)$
The same process gives: $$p = N_V e^{(\frac{-(E_F-E_V)}{k_BT})}$$
Where $N_V$ is the effective density of states in the valence band
$N_V = 2(\frac{m_h^* k_B T}{2\pi \hbar ^2})^{3/2}$

Even if we dope the material and change n or p, the product of the two always remains the same for a given semiconductor at a constant temperature. $np=n_i^2$
$n_i^2 = np = N_CN_V e^{(\frac{-E_g}{k_bT})}$. $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier density, which is independent of the position of the Fermi level

Law of mass action - $np =$ constant

In an instrinsic semiconductor, n must be equal to p: $n=p=n_i$
Equating n and p we find that $E_{Fi} = \frac{E_C+E_V}{2}+\frac{k_BT}{2}\ln(N_V/N_C)$. In first approximation the second term is negligible.
Therefore $E_{Fi} = \frac{E_C+E_V}{2}$

Doping -
Semiconductors that have been doped are called extrinsic
Acceptors are impurity atoms used to achieve p-type doping

For silicon, the doping is added in a concentration of about 1 part in $10^6$
Group V impurities generate an energy level, known as the donor level $E_D$ in the gap, lying just $0.01eV$ below the conduction band edge $E_C$. It will be fully occupied at $0K$

The material is still electrically neutral. When the electron is excited into the CB it leaves behind a positively charged ion. The donor is now ionised, but does not contribute to conduction as fixed in the crystal lattice.

In n-type material the electrons are the majority carriers, whereas in a p-type material it is the holes which are the majority carriers
$$\sigma = ne\mu_e+pe\mu_h$$

Doping in III-V semiconductors -
How can we dope III-V semiconductors like GaAs which already contains atoms from groups III and V?
We can use transition metals and group IV elements, which act as p-type or n-type dopants depending on their chemical preference for substituting for the Ga or the As

In the most general case: $p+(N_D-n_D)=n+(N_A-p_A)$

The Hall Effect -
A particle of charge $q$ moving with velocity $v$ in a magnetic field $B$ will experience a Lorentz force: $\textbf{F}=q\textbf{v}\times \textbf{B}$
The Hall effect exploits this Lorentz force to determine both the sign and density of the majority carriers in a semiconductor
![[Pasted image 20260923180806.png]]
We apply a magnetic field $B_z$ perpendicular to the flow of a current $J_x$. The charge carriers experience a Lorentz force $F_y$ perpendicular to both $J_x$ and $B_z$.
We measure the electric field $E_y$ established in the y-direction
