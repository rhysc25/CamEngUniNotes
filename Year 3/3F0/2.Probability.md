PDF is the derivative of the CDF - $f(x)=\frac{dF(X)}{dx}$
$\int_{x=0}^1 f(x) dx = 1$

Binomial distribution - number of occurances
$P[(\sum_1^2 X_i)=k] = 3Ck \cdot p^k (1-p)^{3-k}$

Perform an action until you get a specific desired outcome - Geometric distribution
$P(n = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p$

Poisson - number of occurrences per unit time
$P(X=k)=\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$

The interval between these occurrences is an exponential distribution

Beta distribution - flexibly parameterised distribution

Normal distribution - $\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma})^2}$
Central limited theorem - many random variables from the same distribution approaches a normal distribution

$P(A)=P(A\cap B) \cup P(A\cap \overline{B})$
$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$

Multivariable -
Marginalisation: $f_{X_1}(x_1)=\int f_{X_1,X_2}(x_1,x_2)dx_x$

Cov$(X,Y)=E[(X-E(X))(Y-E(Y))]$

What about a distribution for the sum of two random variables:
$P_{X+Y}(k)=P(X+Y=k)=\sum_i P_X(i)P_Y(k-i)$
This is a convolution. In the continuous case it is an integral

Moment generating function $M_X(s)=E[e^{sx}]$
When you take the nth derivative of the moment generating function and look at it at $s=0$, you will get the nth moment of X
Another useful relation: $M_{X+Y}(s)=M_X(s)\cdot M_Y(s)$

Random processes -
A random walk is a discrete time process
Brownian motion is a continuous time process

Markovity: A process is Markovian if the future only depends on the present, not the past
Stationarity: A stationary process means the distribution at a given time is the same distribution at a time in the future
Ergodicity: We can average over time, as well as over trials at the same time

Extra tools for analysis in the time dimension - 
* Autocovariance
* Cross covariance
* Power spectral density function
* Cross spectral density function
