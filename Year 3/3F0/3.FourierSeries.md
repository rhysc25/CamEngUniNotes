Consider waves as $e^{jt}=\cos(t)+j\sin(t)$

If a wave has period T, $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$. $\omega_k(t)=\frac{1}{T}e^{jk\omega_0 t}$ is an infinite family of functions which are T periodic

If we are discrete-time, we only have a finite number of N-periodic signals 
$\omega_k[n]=\frac{1}{N}e^{jk\omega_0 n}$ for $k\in \{0,...,N-1\}$

We then define the nth root of unity: $w = e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}}$
With these we can create vector representations of the N-periodic discrete-time exponential signals

$w_k = [w^{k0}...w^{n(N-1)}]^T$
We can stack these in an NxN Fourier matrix

For the 4x4 Fourier matrix:
The first column is a constant signal, so just 1s
The second column is it moving a quarter of the unit circle every time step
The third is half the circle
The fourth is 3 quarters

The DFT of a sequence $x_n$ is defined by:
$X_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_ne^{-i2\pi kn/N}$
With the matrix representation:
$\textbf{X}=W^* \textbf{x}$
And the inverse DFT is $\textbf{x}=\frac{1}{N}W\textbf{X}$

When you perform the DFT you are dotting the input with rows representing different frequencies of movement around the unit circle, so finding the size of component which matches that frequency

Four different Fourier transforms correspond to four distinct temporal domains:
Period and aperiodic
Discrete-time and continous-time

The only one you can program is periodic and discrete-time: T = Z mod N

We can think of $x_n$ as the temporal coordinates of the signal, the values of shifted impulses:
$x[n]=x_0\delta[n]+x_1 \delta[n-1] +...+x_{N-1}\delta[n-(N-1)]$

$X_k$ is then the frequency coordinates of the signal, expressed as a linear combination of the N-periodic exponentials
$x[n]=X_0w_o[n]+X_1w_1[n]+...+X_{N-1}w_{N-1}[n]$

Orthogonality of Fourier vectors:
The N Fourier vectors are orthogonal to each other, so they form an orthogonal basis of the vector space $C^N$

$\frac{1}{N}WW^* = I_N$

We can also think of the Fourier coordinates as the projection of x onto the kth Fourier basis
$X_k=\frac{<x[.],w_k[.]>}{<w_k[.],w_k[.]>}$, where these are complex inner products

Parseval Theorem
$<\textbf{x},\textbf{y}>=\frac{1}{N}<\textbf{X},\textbf{Y}>$
Apart from a scaling factor, the Fourier transform preserves angles between vectors, so it is a unitary transformation (rotation)
It also does not change the energy of the signal

Convolution Theorem
Convolution in the time domain is multiplication in the Fourier domain.

Convolution between DT periodic signals is called circular convolution 
$y = h \circ u$ defined by $y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[(n-k)modN]u[k]$

$Y_k=H_kU_k$

Fast Fourier Transform
Because the Fourier matrix is made of the complex roots of unity, it has a special sparse factorisation, which takes complexity form $N^2$ to $NlogN$

If we move to continuous-time periodic signals, we get an infinite number of coefficients
For this we have to adapt our definition of the inner product $<f(.),g(.)>=\int_T f(t)g^*(t)dt$

To go to aperiodic CT signals:
We think of an aperiodic signal as the limit of a sequence of T-periodic signals as $T\rightarrow \infty$

From this we get:
$X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt$
And the inverse:
$x(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}X(\omega)e^{j\omega t}d\omega$

Sampling a CT signal results in a DT signal
Interpolating the samples of a DT signal results in a CT signal
Windowing a periodic signal results in an aperiodic signal

To reduce our four signal spaces to one: think fo DT signals as samples of CT ones, and think of periodic signals as limits of aperiodic signals with a growing window

They are all limits of aperiodic CT signals

The Fourier transform of a window is a sinc and the Fourier transform of a sinc is a window

Expanding one reduces the size of the other
So as we grow our window we shrink the sinc until we get an impulse
$\delta(.)$ is the limit of sinc

Therefore the transform of a constant signal 1 is an impulse. Any signal with infinite energy will converge to an impulse in the Fourier domain

A DT sampled signal has the CT representation:
$x_s(t)=\sum_{n\in Z}x(nT)\delta(t-nT)$
and so:
$X_s{\omega}=\sum_{n\in Z}x(nT)e^{-jn\omega T}$
