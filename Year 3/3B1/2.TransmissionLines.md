When we are working with radio frequency circuits, the wavelengths can be sufficiently small that impedance and reflections are important to maintaining signal integrity.
PCB tracks are to be considered as transmission lines

RF modules such as oscillators, mixers, transformers, etc. are often matches to 50$\Omega$, hence the signal tracks should be too, or impedance matching networks needed at the joins

The characteristic impedance for a coaxial cable is $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}\ln\frac{a}{b}$

Stripline has the track going through the material
Microstrip has it going on the material, easier for SMT and to probe

Let's examine a simple model of a microstrip line
The capacitance per unit length of the line may be estimated by $C=\frac{(w+2d)}{d}\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$
Here we have approximated the effective width of the copper track to be the actual width plus a dielectric thickness each side, to account for fringing effects.

This gives $Z_0=\frac{d}{w+2d}\frac{1}{C_0\epsilon_0\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}$

The general solution to the telegrapher's equations for a sinusoidal wave is the sum of forwards and backwards travelling waves $V_F$ and $V_B$. $V_F/V_B=Z_0$

Reflection coefficient $\Gamma_0 = \frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$
The reflection looking into the line is a function of the length of the line
Much of RF engineering is managing impedance mismatch, which can be done by adjusting the load. The smith chart helps with these calculations graphically.
$\Gamma(l)=Z_0\frac{Z_L+jZ_0\tan\beta l}{Z_0+jZ_L\tan \beta l}$

Antennas - 
Poynting vector $\textbf{P}=\frac{1}{2}\textbf{E}\times \textbf{H}^*$. Power flow is orthogonal to both the E and H fields which are both mutually orthogonal

Suppose we are working at such a high frequency that there is a phase change around the loop and that the loop is about a quarter of a wavelength across. If we look at the far field contribution to the magnetic field from point A and point B, because of the phase shift in the current and the additional propagation delay, the fields are in phase and add, hence it is now radiating energy.

The ideal dipole - 
A tiny radiating current element
Small element on surface $dS=rd\theta r\sin \theta d\phi \hat{r}=r^2\sin\theta d\theta d\phi\hat{r}$

Important quantities -
Radiation resistance $R_r$: Power radiated = $P_r=\frac{1}{2}I^2R$, where $I$ is peak current
Radiation efficiency, e: $e = \frac{P_r}{P_{in}}$, where $P_{in}=\frac{1}{2}I^2 (R_r+R_{ohmic})$
Gain, G: $G=\frac{\text{max power radiated per unit area}}{\text{isotropic power per unit area}}$, where the isotropic power per unit area is $\frac{P_{in}}{4\pi r^2}$
Directivity, D: $D=\frac{\text{Gain}}{\text{Radiation effeciency}}=\frac{G}{e}$
Effective Aperture/Area, $A_e$: Power delivered into a matched load by an antenna = $A_e\times\text{power density in incident radio wave}$

$G=\frac{4\pi A_e}{\lambda^2}$

Skin depth - the thickness of region where current can be considered to be carried in a conductor. $\delta =\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega \mu \sigma}}$

Microstrip Patch - 
A length of microstrip can be made to resonate as a $\lambda/2$ dipole, supporting a standing wave end to end, excited by an off-centre microstrip feed.

Oscillators -
Almost every electronic product contains an oscillator. The simplest form of oscillator is the relaxation oscillator, which works by charging up a capacitor through a resistor and subsequently discharging it when a threshold is reached.
The threshold detector must have hysteresis, which is easily achieved with positive feedback, such as in a Schmidt trigger gate.
