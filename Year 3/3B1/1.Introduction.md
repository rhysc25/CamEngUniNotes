1-10mA is a good DC bias current

A typical transmitter has an AF amplifier, a modulator to add the carrier, an RF amplifier (works at higher frequencies), and an impedance matching circuit
A typical receiver has impedance matching, RF amplifer, tuning and demodulation, AF amplifier and a speaker

AM - amplitude of the main carrier is varied with the magnitude of the data signal
FM - frequency of the main carrier is swung up and down about its mean by the data signal
Phase Shift Modulation/Keying (PSM/PSK) - phase shifts are introduced according to the data signal. For digital data we can use a phase shift of 0 and 180 degrees.

The simplest receiver consistes of an LC resonant circuit to pick out the right frequency, then a diode and an RC circuit for envelope detection.
Drawbacks - 
Low power output as no gain
There is a limited tuning range as the capacitor can be varied $20:1$, which means the frequency can be varied $4:1$ as $f\propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}$
Low selectivity as the Q factor is rather low

Better and more common receiver - Superhet receiver. It consists of:
Matching and pre-tuning, a local oscillator and mixer, and IF filter and amplifier, a demodulator and an AF amplifier

The mixer is basically an analogue voltage multupler which produces an outut voltage equal to the product of the 2 input voltages
$\sin A \times \sin B = 1/2 \cos (A-B) - 1/2 \cos(A+B)$
The output signal comprises the pair of sum and difference frequencies
One of these is discarded by filtering, so the mixer moves the selected carrier frequency down to a fixed Intermediate Frequency (IF) at which the rest of the circuit operates

Purpose of the pre-tuning stage: If the desired IF is x, then we can make x by $f_1-f_2$ where $f_1$ is the local oscillator frequency, but we can also make it with the image frequency $f_3 - f_1$.
Therefore we need pre tuning to filter out $f_3$, whatever it may be.

Therefore we must make the following components:
Amplifiers, modulates and demodulates, oscillators and filters, matching circuits, antennas

Ebers-Moll Model of a BJT
This applies to large and small signals
$I_C = I_S [e^{q\frac{V_{BE}}{kT}}-1] \approx I_S e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_t}}$ MEMORISE THISi
$V_t$ here is the thermal voltage, $\frac{kT}{q}$
![[Pasted image 20261013075405.png]]
You can see how it's roughly exponential in shape

This is a similar form to the PN diode equation where for the base-emitter diode -
$I_B = I_S' [e^{q\frac{V_{BE}}{kT}}-1] \approx I_S' e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_t}}$

From this is is clear that $h_{FE} = \frac{I_S}{I_S'}$
As well as this, $h_{fe}=\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial I_B}$. If you run through the calculation with these simplified Ebers Moll equations, you get that $h_{fe}=h_{FE}$

There is an internal emitter resistance associated with BJTs which depends on the current flowing through the device $r_e \approx \frac{dV_{BE}}{dI_C}=\frac{kT}{qI_C}\approx \frac{25}{I_C}$ with $I_C$ in $mA$, at room temperature

In general, as temperature T increases, so does $h_{fe}$. This increase in gain then increases $T$, so we can get thermal runaway if not mitigated

In the BJT small signal model we can often ignore the base-emitter voltage source that represents the Early Effect (collector current increases with collector-emitter voltage due to increased gain) and we can also ignore the collector-emitter conductance as often negligible compared to the collector load

Useful approximation - replace the base resistor model with an emitter resistance model
![[Pasted image 20261013160532.png]]
Before $v_{be}$ was $i_bh_{ie}$, now it is $h_{fe}i_br_e$, so we can conclude that:
$r_e \approx h_{ie}/h_{fe} = \frac{V_t}{I_C}$
It is clear that both $r_e$ and $h_{ie}$ are $\propto 1/I_C$ 

We cannot just add a load to any amplifier circuit and expect it to function as intended. Even if we impedance match, we lose 1/2 of the signal at both input and output

Summary of Steps to Design a Simple Bipolar Transistor Amplifier - 
1) How many stages required at 20dB power gain per transitor
2) Voltage gain required from each stage
3) Ratio of input to output impedance for each stage
4) Choose R4s
5) Choose R3s
6) choose R2s and R1s
7) Select transistors for high $h_{fe}$, voltage, current and power ratings
8) Check effect of low $h_{fe}$, particularly for higher current stage

How to compute these:
1) The power gain is $10\log_{10}(\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}})$.  $P_{in}$ can be found by $\frac{1}{2}V_{rms}^2/(R_{in}+R_{source})$. The half is needed due to impedance matching, half of the power is lost in the source
2) We calculate the total voltage gain by assuming that we have perfect impedance matching so a decrease of a half for every coupling 
3) To work out $R_{in}/R_{out}$ ratio, consider the ratio of source and load. Source impedance / load impedance. Then raise this to $\frac{1}{\text{num stages}}$ power to give you $R_{in}/R_{out}$ for each stage
4) $R_{4}$ is chosen to match $R_{out}$ as for our amplifier $R_{out} = R_4$ for each stage
5) Choose $R_3$ as we know Gain $\approx R_4/R_3$
6) Choose $R_2$ as the nearest standard value to $2\times R_{in}$
7) As an approximation $V_E\approx V_S / 20$ and $V_B=(V_E + 0.6$. Find $R_1$ using the fact that $V_CC R_2 / (R_2 + R_1) = V_B$. Verify your values with the $R_{in}$ formula
We then choose the transistor with the highest $h_{fe}$ that can handle $I_C,V_S$.
The last stage must be a high power transistor

The last thing we need to consider is the frequency roll off of each stage due to our coupling capacitors. The value would be increasing as stage number increases

* We should also check the effect of $r_e$: The gain is now $R4 / (R3 + r_e)$
* We should also check the effect of a decreased $h_{fe}$: a smaller $h_{fe}$ reduces the input impedance. If we have given a gain margin on all our stages, this shouldn't be too big a problem. A lower $h_{fe}$ also requires more base current. The resistors must provide this without appreciable voltage drop, so the values of $R2$ and $R1$ must decrease
* We should also check the effect of a $V_{BE}\neq 0.6$: For this we use the Ebers Moll equation to find $I_S$. Then we iteratively solve $V_{BE}=V_B-V_E=1.2-1.2I_C=1.2-1.2\times 2.55\times 10^{-12}e^{V_{BE}/0.025}$
Most of the time, we find no big error, because of the negative feedback of $R3$

Transistor Frequency Response - 
If we have a capacitance across the gain C, then the input capacitance is equivalent to $C_i = (1+G)C$ - the Miller Effect
This can be proven by considering:
![[Pasted image 20261015144255.png]]
$i_i=\frac{v_i - v_o}{\frac{1}{j\omega c}}=\frac{v_i + Gv_i}{\frac{1}{j\omega c}}=\frac{v_i}{\frac{1}{j\omega(1+G)C}}$
The output capacitance $C_o = (1+\frac{1}{G})C$ 

In practice, transistors have various capacitances between their terminals ($C_{ie}, C_{oe}, C_{cb}$) where $C_{ie}$ is the largest

$C_{ie}$ is proportional to $I_E$ as the width in the pn junction changes with bias
A good figure of merit is therefore $f_t$, representing useful bandwidth
$f_t=\frac{1}{0.05\pi k}$ where $k$ is the constant of proportionality $C_{ie}=kI_E\approx kI_C$
Hence the main gian and frequency characteristics of a transistor can be described by $h_{fe}$ and $f_t$

The frequency response roll off is dominated by the Miller effect

To circumvent the Miller effect in a differential amplifier, ground one base and take the output from that collector, as this means the low input impedance to that base gives a short RC time constant
Use the other base as input, so we can short the collector resistor

Mixers -
The desired output frequency is usually the sum or difference of the original frequencies
We can achieve this by multiplying two sinusoids together
$\sin(\omega_1 t)\times \sin(\omega_2 t)=\frac{1}{2}\cos((\omega_1-\omega_2)t)-\frac{1}{2}\cos((\omega_1+\omega_2)t)$
For this purpose, we need non-linear components

Properties of an ideal mixer:
* Isolation of the LO, IF, RF
* No harmonic distortion
* Low conversion loss between frequencies

A simple mixer switches between inverting and non-inverting at double the frequency of the incoming wave, to do full-wave rectification

Alternatively, we could use a diode ring to perform this rectification

For cases where a diode ring is not required or its expense is not justifiable, the cascode or dual-gate MOSFET circuits may be usitilised

Phase Locked Loop -

We often need to convert one frequency to another -
![[Pasted image 20260916143837.png]]

The phase-detector (mixer) compares the phase of the input with the voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) output and produces a phase error signal with a dc component. 
This signal is filtered and fed to the VCO such that the frequency and phase of the VCO output locks to that of the input signal and will track it. This gives a sort of flywheel effect, good for if the input disappears for a while.

The lock-up time, the time for the VCO to match the input signal, is given roughly by: $t\approx \frac{4\Delta \omega ^ 2}{B^3}$
$\Delta \omega$ is the initial frequency difference
B is the loop bandwidth = $K_0K_PK_F$

Demodulation Circuits -
For AM, we can use a simple diode and low pass filter circuit. Also, with a forward bias on the diode, the demodulation at low signal levels is improved.

For FM, we could use the PLL already covered. Other techniques include band edge filters which convert FM to AM.
