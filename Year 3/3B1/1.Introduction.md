A typical transmitter has an AF amplifier, a modulator to add the carrier, an RF amplifier (works at higher frequencies), and an impedance matching circuit
A typical receiver has impedance matching, RF amplifer, tuning and demodulation, AF amplifier and a speaker

AM - amplitude of the main carrier is varied with the magnitude of the data signa;
FM - frequency of the main carrier is swung up and down about its mean by the data signal
Phase Shift Modulation/Keying (PSM/PSK) - phase shifts are introduced according to the data signal

The simplest receiver consistes of an LC resonant circuit to pick out the right frequency, then a diode and an RC circuit for envelope detection.
Drawbacks - Low power output and bandwidth varies with frequency

Better and more common receiver - Superhet receiver. It consists of:
Matching and pre-tuning, a local oscillator and mixer, and IF filter and amplifier, a demodulator and an AF amplifier

The mixer is basically an analogue voltage multupler which produces an outut voltage equal to the product of the 2 input voltages
$\sin A \times \sin B = 1/2 \cos (A-B) - 1/2 \cos(A+B)$
The output signal comprises the pair of sum and difference frequencies
One of these is discarded by filtering, so the mixer moves the selected carrier frequency down to a fixed Intermediate Frequency (IF) at whcih the rest of the circuit operates

Therefore we must make the following components:
Amplifiers, modulates and demodulates, oscillators and filters, matching circuits, antennas

Ebers-Moll Model of a BJT
$I_C = I_S [e^{q\frac{V_{BE}}{kT}}-1] \approx I_S e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_t}}$

There is an internal emitter resistance associated with BJTs which depends on the current flowing through the device $r_e \approx \frac{dV_{BE}}{dI_C}=\frac{kT}{qI_C}\approx \frac{25}{I_C}$ with $I_C$ in $mA$, at room temperature

In the BJT small signal model we can often ignore the base-emitter voltage source that represents the Early Effect (collector current increases with collector-emitter voltage due to increased gain) and we can also ignore the collector-emitter conductance as often negligible comapred to the collector load

Transistor Frequency Response - 
If we have a capacitance across the gain C, then the input capacitance is equivalent to $C_i = (1+G)C$ - the Miller Effect
The output capacitance $C_o = (1+\frac{1}{G})C$ 

In practice, transistors have various capacitances between their terminals ($C_{ie}, C_{oe}, C_{cb}$) where $C_{ie}$ is the largest

$C_{ie}$ is proportional to $I_E$ as the width in the pn junction changes with bias
A good figure of merit is therefore $f_t$, representing useful bandwidth
$f_t=\frac{1}{0.05\pi k}$ where $k$ is the constant of proportionality $C_{ie}=kI_E\approx kI_C$
Hence the main gian and frequency characteristics of a transistor can be described by $h_{fe}$ and $f_t$

The frequency response roll off is dominated by the Miller effect

To circumvent the Miller effect in a differential amplifier, ground one base and take the output from that collector, as this means the low input impedance to that base gives a short RC time constant
Use the other base as input, so we can short the collector resistor

Mixers -
The desired output frequency is usually the sum or difference of the original frequencies
We can achieve this by multiplying two sinusoids together
$\sin(\omega_1 t)\times \sin(\omega_2 t)=\frac{1}{2}\cos((\omega_1-\omega_2)t)-\frac{1}{2}\cos((\omega_1+\omega_2)t)$
For this purpose, we need non-linear components

Properties of an ideal mixer:
* Isolation of the LO, IF, RF
* No harmonic distortion
* Low conversion loss between frequencies

A simple mixer switches between inverting and non-inverting at double the frequency of the incoming wave, to do full-wave rectification

Alternatively, we could use a diode ring to perform this rectification

For cases where a diode ring is not required or its expense is not justifiable, the cascode or dual-gate MOSFET circuits may be usitilised
