The controller is just proportional in this example. We want to know for which values of K is the closed loop stable?

We assume that the system is causal

Feedback can also be used to stabilise an unstable system, or to improve the stability and convergence.

A plant is a linear system with transfer function $G(z)$
$X(z)=U(z)-Y(z)=U(z)-KG(z)X(z)$
$X(z)=\frac{U(z)}{1+KG(z)}$
In this course we consider return ratio $L(z)$ to be $G(z)$

The transfer function of the closed loop system is $H(z)=\frac{KG(z)}{1+KG(z)}$

For stability, the denominator is the one that predicts stability

The Nyquist diagram-
We predict the stability of the closed loop from the open loop transfer function
Nyquist diagram: $L(e^{j\theta})$ drawn in the complex plane as $\theta$ increases from $0$ to $2\pi$ (or $-\pi$ to $\pi$)

In lecture series - blue curve is positive frequencies
Need arrows on our Nyquist diagram!!

The open loop transfer function is the transfer function around the loop excluding K

For a real system $L(e^{-j\theta})=\overline{L(e^{j\theta})}$. The remainder of the Nyquist diagram is therefore obtained by reflection in the real axis.

We could determine stability from the poles of $H(z)$

Nyquist Stability Criterion -
The number $N_{op,o}$ of open loop poles strictly outside the unit circle = number of counter clockwise encirclements of $-1/K$ by $L(e^{j\theta})$ as $\theta$ increases from $-\pi$ to $\pi$
THE DIRECTION OF ENCIRCLEMENTS MATTER

Open loop poles ON the unit circle are delicate, we will return to them later

Flow diagram -
If the poles are not on the unit circle - determine the required encirclements of $-1/K$. If there is a stable region, determine the real axis intersections, the range for $-1/K$ then the range for $K$. Then optionally verify for values of K

Calculating the intersection with the real axis - finding when the imaginary part of $L(e^{j\theta}) = 0$ 

What do the encirclements tell us about the poles and zeros? 
For any rational function $F(z)$ the number of counter clockwise encirclements of the origin by a Nyquist trajectory tells us about the poles and zeros

$\angle F(e^{j\theta})=\angle (c)+\sum_{k=1}^m \angle (e^{j\theta}-z_k)-\sum_{k=1}^n \angle (e^{j\theta}-p_k)$
A zero inside the unit circle will cause an encirclement as we're essentially travelling around it

For a zero/pole on the unit circle the phase jumps by $\pi(-\pi)$ when it passes the zero (pole)
A zero (pole) on the unit circle causes an encirclement just the same as a zero (pole) inside the unit circle, but there is a discontinuity in angle

$N_{z,i}$ - the number of zeros of F(z) inside the unit circle
$N_{p,i}$ - the number of poles of F(z) inside the unit circle
The overall increase $\alpha$ of $\angle F(e^{j\theta})$ as $\theta$ increases from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ is $2\pi (N_{z,i}-N_{p,i})$

Net encirclements = $C=N_{z,i}-N_{p,i}$
If $1+KL()$ encircles 0, then $L()$ encircles $-1/K$

Proof of Nyquist stability criterion -
Property 1 - Encirclement property - 
If the zero is inside the unit circle the angle will increase by $2\pi$, therefore encirclement. If it is outside, then the increase in $\angle(e^{j\theta}-z_i)$ is 0

The effect of a pole would be a negative encirclement

Therefore the net number of encirclements = $C=N_{z,i}-N_{p,i}$

Property 2 - Polynomial degrees
$L(z) = b(z)/a(z)$. $deg[b(z)] \leq deg[a(z)]$
The transfer function of the closed loop system - $H(z)= \frac{N(z)}{1+KL(z)}$ where the numerator depends on the configuration
$1+KL(z) = \frac{a(z)+Kb(z)}{a(z)}$

UNLESS $a_0 + Kb_0 = 0, deg[a(z)+Kb(z)]=deg[a(z)]$. Assume not for the moment

Fundamental theorem of algebra: a degree $l$ polynomial has $l$ complex roots, including repeated
$1+KL(z)$ has $l$ poles and $l$ zeros

The zeros of $1+KL(z)$ are the poles of the closed loop system
The poles of $1+KL(z)$ are the poles of $L(z)$
Therefore: The closed-loop system $H(z)$ and the open-loop system $L(z)$ have the same number $l$ of poles

Property 3 - Critical point
$L(e^{j\theta})$ encircles $-1/K$

Combining property 1 and 2:
Num poles of $L(z)$ inside + num poles of $L(z)$ outside =
Num poles of $H(z)$ inside + num poles of $H(z)$ outside

With a bunch of manipulation we can get that:
Num encirclements of $-1/K$ by $L(e^{j\theta}) =$ num poles of $L(z)$ outside

This hides two delicate cases:
1) $a(z)+Kb(z)$ has lower degree than $a(z)$
2) Poles or zeros on the unit circle

(1) If the denominator degree n is smaller than that of the numerator, this is an illegal transfer function because it doesn't correspond to the delta response of a system via the one-sided z transform
It's only a problem if $K=-1/b_0$ is within the range that we declared to be stable

Assume a K close to $-1/b_0$, so $K+1/b_0 = \epsilon$
Hence: $H(z) = \frac{1}{\epsilon b_0}\cdot \frac{a(z)N(z)}{z^n+ \frac{1}{\epsilon b_0}((a_1 + Kb_1)z^{n-1}+...)}$
All except the leading coefficient of the denominator polynomial scale with $1/\epsilon$
This can only happen if at least one pole goes to infinity, so unstable, so we don't care about it

(2) For the proof of the Nyquist we can include zeros/poles on the unit circle as being inside the unit circle.
The inequalities defining the stable range of K are strict inequalities.

The open loop poles on the unit circle will cause the Nyquist diagram to go to infinity as it pass through the pole on the unit circle

In order to obtain a closed curve for the Nyquist locus and hence correctly count the encirclements, it is customary to indent the path of z around the poles on the unit circle with a small semi-circluar excursion outside the unit circle. Then the open loop poles on the unit circle are counted as being stable in the stability criterion.

Asymptotes -
if there is an open-loop pole of multiplicity one on the unit circle then the Nyquist diagram will be asymptotic to a straight line as it tends to infinity.
Suppose we have a pole at $z=1$:
$G(z)=\frac{1}{z-1}F(z)$. Expand $F(z)$ as a Taylor series. E.g.
$G(z)\approx \frac{F(1)}{z-1}+F'(1)$

$G(e^{j\theta}) \approx -1/2 F(1) + F'(1) -j...$
So the asymptote has real part $-1/2F(1) +F'(1)$
Nice formula, but only works if the pole is at $1$!!

More general:
Suppose that $L(z)$ has a pole of order m at $z_0 = e^{j\theta_0}$
$L(e^{j(\theta_0 + \delta)})=\frac{F(z_0)}{z_0^m (j\delta)^m}(1+O(\delta))$
For poles with m larger than 1 you cannot get a straight line asymptote

The Nyquist curce approaches the straight line: $C+tD$
Where $D=-\frac{jF(z_0)}{z_0}$ and $C=F'(z_0)-\frac{F(z_0)}{2z_0}$
